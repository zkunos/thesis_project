\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
Firstly, the study was based in a single centre. All data were obtained from one rehabilitation unit. Therefore, the possibility of selection bias exists and extrapolation to other clinical groups cannot be made. Although all tests used have been extensively studied and have international validity, the six cognitive domains provide a common framework for the description of post-injury cognition. However, the validation of this cognitive severity structure will require multi-centre studies prior to its acceptance as a general characteristic of ABI rather than as a specific characteristic of this unit.


Secondly, most analyses treat 17{,}406 assessments as observations rather than grouping them within the 7{,}285 patients. Repeated follow-up assessments from one patient are correlated, yet the primary clustering and assessment-level bootstrap do not model that dependence. Retaining all assessments describes the clinical records available at different recovery stages, but it may inflate apparent cluster stability and produce confidence intervals that are too narrow. The patient-grouped multinomial sensitivity analysis addresses H3. The new extension adds patient-level discovery/validation, patient subsampling and integrated random-effects trajectories, but it does not retroactively correct uncertainty intervals from the historical assessment-level analysis.



Thirdly, the recovered structure is a limitation on interpretation. The historical findings are consistent with a broad severity gradient, while the extension shows that reproducible partitions remain sensitive to representation. The available analyses do not establish a unique categorical structure. Most of the cohort fall along a single dimension representing overall cognitive severity with PC1 accounting for 56\% of the variance. The three tiers represent a discretization of this continuum. Therefore, the tiers should not be regarded as phenotypic entities with well defined boundaries. Tier count will be influenced by both the resolution of clustering and the proportion of missing data. Assessments located near tier boundaries should be viewed cautiously. Ultimately, a continuous severity score may be more representative than discrete tiers (Section~\ref{sec:future}).



Fourthly, the analytical approach has limited capacity to model temporal change. ABI recovery is typically rapid during the early stages and follows a slower rate thereafter \parencite{ponsford2014longitudinal,maas2017traumatic}. Therefore, a single assessment provides only a 'snapshot' of a patient's recovery status. For many patients in the dataset, the inclusion of multiple assessments allows for the examination of change within patients (below); however, the timing of follow-up assessments is still determined by clinical necessity rather than being standardized.



Of the 5{,}206 patients with longitudinal data (Section~\ref{sec:robustness}), 50.2\% retain the same tier across visits. Among the narrower subset of 912 patients with exactly two assessments and a tier change, 706 move toward higher-scoring bands and 206 toward lower-scoring bands. This pattern is compatible with recovery, but tier movement is not a functional outcome. Selection, discharge, visit timing and changes in test availability may also contribute.



Future longitudinal designs with standardized assessment intervals will be necessary to determine this question.



The imputation strategy introduces another constraint on interpretation. The MAR assumption cannot be tested from observed data alone \parencite{rubin1976inference,little2019statistical}. A test may be incomplete because a patient cannot perform it, making its missing value informative. Under such MNAR mechanisms, imputation could overestimate performance. The current comparisons do not establish an upper bound on this bias. A formally specified delta-shift sensitivity analysis is needed to assess departures from the working missingness model.



Structured co-occurrence of missingness is compatible with test-battery protocols, but does not distinguish protocol-driven from outcome-dependent non-completion. The comparison of ten imputation methods gives at least 80\% assignment agreement for 86.2\% of assessments under a fixed MICE-fitted rule. This supports conditional assignment robustness; it does not establish that missingness is ignorable or that independently fitted pipelines recover the same groups.



Assessment eligibility required at least one observed retained test, allowing records with substantial missingness to enter the analysis. The stricter seven-observed-test sensitivity retained 6{,}602 index patients and gave ARI 0.903 against the reference three-group assignments on shared validation patients. This supports some robustness to sparse-record exclusion, while leaving the unobserved cognitive status of excluded encounters unresolved.



The new observed-data mixtures integrate missing coordinates directly, addressing a limitation of the historical impute-then-cluster baseline. However, their missingness assumptions remain ignorable, their continuous distributions approximate bounded and discrete scores, and their dispersion floor regularizes singular solutions. Four of 32 fits failed the requested convergence tolerance, and both selected $t$ families reached the eight-component boundary. A unique class count therefore remains unidentified. Skew-$t$ and multivariate contaminated longitudinal models were not implemented.



Feature-engineering remains relevant. Non-cognitive features are eliminated and every feature is standardized (direction aligned), and winsorized prior to summarizing them as composites and generating potentially artificial profile differences. More generally, the methodological lesson is clear: unsupervised phenotyping relies on preprocessing. Any distinctive phenotype identified in an exploratory fashion should be examined relative to choices about scaling, variable selection and cohort definition before it receives a clinical label.



HDBSCAN also introduces sensitivity to hyperparameters. The search evaluates \(9\times120\) configurations and selects the composite quality metric \(Q\). Because \(Q\) contains silhouette and the UMAP seed is selected partly on silhouette, the reported embedding separation is optimistic by construction. It is a selected internal statistic rather than an unbiased estimate of cluster quality. PCA provides complementary evidence of a dominant severity axis (PC1 \(=56\%\)), while the historical assessment-subsampling mean ARI of 0.273 shows that exact assignments are sensitive to resampling. Neither finding establishes a uniquely correct categorical partition.



Computational cost presents a further practical limit. Running UMAP + HDBSCAN fifty times per method is computationally intensive; however, due to modularity allowing for parallel processing, runtime costs were manageable on a typical consumer-grade laptop.


The extension also has specific validation limits. Feature selection was inherited from the historical full cohort. Equal-domain scaling was introduced after inspecting discovery variances, so it is an exploratory sensitivity. High ARI across bounded Bayesian imputations remains conditional on that model and does not resolve MNAR. Test availability is associated with cluster membership, and neither domain weighting is clinically validated. The Gaussian-copula reference is estimated from these data and resamples missingness independently of values; its tail fractions are descriptive rather than an omnibus calibrated significance test. The separated positive control is not a power study, and a calibrated Silverman test was not implemented. Consensus summaries formed from validation assignment vectors are not separately validated prediction models.

Finally, the earlier supplementary multiple-imputation and complete-case comparisons used raw-test aggregation, whereas the patient-level extension aligns test direction and scale before aggregation. The controlled comparisons quantify sensitivity to this difference but do not recalculate all earlier statistics under the standardized-domain specification. Those earlier statistics therefore cannot validate the patient-level preprocessing pipeline.

\section{Future Directions}\label{sec:future}
Four potential areas for future work arise from the findings and limitations described above.



Firstly, modeling the continuum directly. The most obvious implication of the findings regarding cognitive-severity gradients is that continuous cognitive-severity indices - either derived from PCA or supervised learning models trained on functional-outcome measures - may provide more accurate representations and may be more useful summaries than tier-based classifications. Future research should investigate comparing continuous cognitive-severity indexes to tier classification schemes for predicting prognosis and for assigning rehabilitation intensity according to need by treating tier classification as an interpretable discretization of a continuous measure rather than as natural categories.



Secondly, longitudinal work should extend the implemented linear random-effects mixture. Applying the developed pipeline to assessments collected at regular time points would allow researchers to describe how individual patients' positions on the cognitive-severity gradient evolve over time as part of their recovery trajectories: i.e., whether patients' initial levels predict longer-term outcomes and whether recovery rates differ depending on severity level \parencite{ponsford2014longitudinal}. The present trajectory subset is selected by visit availability and follow-up span; nonlinear change, informative dropout and external functional outcomes remain to be assessed. Its results do not yet establish prognostic or rehabilitation-planning utility.



Thirdly, multi-center validation represents an essential requirement for considering cognitive-severity stratification as a feature of acquired-brain injury rather than as a feature of this specific service. A multi-center study across different health care systems would assess whether the one-dimensional cognitive-severity gradient found here survives changes in catchment population, test batteries, and assessment schedules \parencite{maas2017traumatic}.



Fourthly, integrating neuroimaging will help link cognitive-severity gradings with neural mechanisms. By correlating an individual's position on the cognitive-severity gradient to structural-functional imaging metrics, researchers may identify the neural correlates of global cognitive impairments following ABI; this type of evidence may assist in targeting interventions \parencite{saatman2008classification,maas2017traumatic}.
