\chapter{Conclusions}\label{Chapter7}

\section{Summary of Key Findings}

The purpose of this research project was to determine if stable cognitive representations of acquired brain injury patients could be produced from the test data collected, even when a number of test scores are missing. Both the historical assessment analysis and the patient-level analysis indicated that similar patterns of assessment scores can be produced by consistently applying specific analytical options. However, neither demonstrated the existence of distinct clinical subgroups nor did they demonstrate the lack of such subgroups.

Three severity groupings were developed from 17,406 historical assessments via UMAP+HDBSCAN. Hypothesis H1 receives partial support. All ten imputation methods satisfied both the silhouette and noise criteria for an embedding. Moderate Jaccard agreement existed amongst the various imputation methods; however, the number of clusters differed across methods. A prominent first principal component, together with limited original-domain differentiation, suggests an overall severity interpretation within that representation.

Hypothesis H2 receives support within the fixed-reference design. Using the same MICE-fitted scaler and UMAP model and applying the three-group k-means rule to each of the ten imputed datasets resulted in an average pairwise adjusted Rand index (ARI) of .710. This represents sensitivity to imputation under predefined clustering rules; it does not represent independent discovery of density hierarchies. The historical assessment-level bootstrap ignores patient dependencies. Because the supplementary multiple imputation and complete-case analyses employed sums of raw test scores, their comparability with the standardized domain representations in the extension was limited.

H3 is supported as an association hypothesis (i.e., there is a relationship between clinical unit and tier; Cramér's V was 0.162). The addition of tier to broad diagnostic grouping resulted in a very small decline in patient-grouped validation log loss, however this improvement interval did include zero. H4 is primarily demonstrated through conditioning (i.e., domain aggregation reduced the mean VIF from 2.77 to 1.88 and the condition number from 61.7 to 11.8, while increasing silhouette slightly from 0.473 to 0.481).

\section{Findings from the Patient-Level Extension}

The use of an extension involving 7,285 index patients, divided into two groups (the discovery sample (n=5,828) and the validation sample (n=1,457)), selected two-group k-means in all twenty stochastic-imputation screens. The average pairwise validation ARI was 0.959. The overall ARI for the fixed three-group k-means clustering method averaged 0.935 over imputations and 0.968 over fifty random patient samples. Therefore, there appeared to be reproducible grouping patterns in the cognitive measures that were replicated across samples.

However, the issue of representation is the defining aspect of this problem. When using the equal-domain instead of the robust domain measure for scaling, the classifications produced by the three groupings of data (ARI = 0.243) changed substantially. In addition, after applying the robust domain scaling measure, language and orientation measures explained most of the variability in the data. Also, missing values on memory and executive function tests were found to relate to group membership. High levels of repeatability of the same preprocessing and imputation models do not necessarily translate to stable clinical categories.

The results from using restricted robust $t$-mixtures provided an improved fit to the data as compared to either of the two other Gaussian models. While both selected $t$-families had the same number of components and selected 8 components, the ambiguity in determining the appropriate number of components along with the relative lack of separation among the domains in the six-domain space suggests it may be unrealistic to assign eight unique phenotypic labels. Among the 2,013 patients eligible for longitudinal modelling, extra trajectory classes improved likelihood. However, the introduction of every multi-class trajectory solution resulted in a subgroup having less than the required 5\% expected-membership threshold. Therefore, all that was left under the selection rules was a single class random effects model. Nonlinear variability and small groups remained unaddressed.

\section{Methodological Contribution}

Together, the workflows distinguish robustness under fixed-reference assignment from independent refitting, patient resampling, feature geometry and model fit to observed data. Shared preprocessing aligns test direction and scale before domain aggregation. Original-space metrics, posterior uncertainty, model convergence, conditional consensus and trajectory eligibility are reported explicitly. The implementation was checked numerically; clinical validation requires work beyond this study.

Beginning with the primary cohort, assessments were required to have a minimum of one retained test as the measure for determining whether an assessment contained sufficient test data. In addition to assessing the impact of missing data on clustering, a secondary sensitivity analysis examined the effects of limiting analyses to assessments that included seven or more observed tests to evaluate whether eliminating sparse test profiles would also influence clustering. By making it clear the assessment-completeness rule, this method clearly defines the level of test data required to interpret stability.

\section{Implications for Research and Clinical Interpretation}

Continuous cognitive summaries and descriptive groups may remain candidates for future outcome validation. The current labeling system should include definitions used in preprocessing as well as any degree of uncertainty regarding those labels. These labels must not determine treatment intensities or routes for rehabilitation. Associations with clinical units or repeated cognitive assessments alone will not be sufficient to establish if treatments have produced clinically-relevant outcomes; however, CogDash presents both alternative designs for analyzing research and limitations for evaluating results and making comparisons among profiles from models that were saved during testing.

Empirical priorities for future research include: (1) comparing independently assessed outcome measurements based on continuous scores versus continuous scores with cluster membership; (2) examining the sensitivity of identified effects to missing data, especially where such data are informative; and (3) examining test-administration procedures. Furthermore, replicating the observed relationship among different sites at different time periods is also an empirical priority for future research. Nonlinear longitudinal modelling and the assessment of adequately measured small subgroups may serve as a foundation for future research that does not assume additional mixture components reflect legitimate patient types.

\section{Final Remarks}

The results of this study found that reproducible groupings could be established by examining cognitive scores. The membership in these groups and the total number of groups found can vary based upon the scale used, how missing data is handled and the choice of model type. While there was evidence in support of an overall structure of cognition, it also provided multiple pathways for additional research into subgroups of cognition. Distinct clinical phenotypes and their added clinical utility remain unconfirmed.
