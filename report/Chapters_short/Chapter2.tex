\section{Acquired Brain Injury and Cognitive Assessment}
Acquired brain injury (ABI) refers to brain damage sustained after birth that is not due to a congenital or degenerative condition \parencite{ukdhsc2022abi}. It includes traumatic and non-traumatic injuries such as traumatic brain injury (TBI), stroke, encephalitis and hypoxia/ischemia. Although there are many different etiologies of ABI, their commonality lies within their shared rehabilitative challenges and outcomes. Cognitive deficits post-injury will frequently influence long term functional status. One way that clinicians assess cognitive deficits that result from these types of injuries is through administering standard, normed neuropsychological tests. Most standard test batteries are designed to assess individuals' abilities across multiple domains of cognition. This includes assessing an individual's ability to visually/spatially process information, process language, engage in tasks requiring executive functions, learn and retain new information, and finally, how quickly an individual can process information. Each domain assessed has practical implications. Examples include, but are not limited to, attention deficit impeding an individual's participation in therapeutic interventions, memory impairments limiting an individual's ability to develop effective compensatory strategies for daily living activities, limitations in executive and language skills contributing to issues with planning, self-advocacy and communicating needs, and visuospatial impairments impacting an individual's ability to independently navigate their environment. Thus, a cognitive profile generated through comprehensive administration of neuropsychological testing can provide valuable information for planning rehabilitation programs, assist in establishing medicolegal issues related to the injury, and inform return-to-work decisions.

People who suffer from moderate to severe traumatic brain injuries (TBI) can have lasting cognitive problems, and there is no one way for people to recover from their injuries. Long-term studies have examined functioning for up to ten years after injury \parencite{ponsford2014longitudinal}. In their review, Rabinowitz and Levin [\citeyear{rabinowitz2014neuropsychological}] describe that the cognitive outcome depends upon a number of factors related to the injury itself as well as the patient's history, including the patient's age at time of injury and pre-injury neuropsychiatric status. Injury severity alone therefore cannot fully describe an individual's cognitive profile.

Neuropsychologists will typically use modified versions of standard test batteries based upon such variables as: what specific questions were posed in the referral; the patient's tolerance for testing; and clinical judgment. Each of these decisions introduces additional variability/structured missingness into the resultant data sets. Therefore, multivariate analyses need to take this structured missingness into account rather than simply viewing the missing values as merely a minor methodological concern.

\section{Missing Data in Clinical Research}
Although missing data occur frequently in clinical research \parencite{gravesteijn2021missing}, three mechanisms are distinguished \textcite{rubin1976inference}: missing completely at random (MCAR), missing at random (MAR) and missing not at random (MNAR). Missing completely at random (MCAR) indicates that missingness is unrelated to all values (both seen and unseen). Missing at random (MAR) implies that missingness may depend upon the observed values, but once those values are accounted for, then there will be no dependence on the missing values. Missing not at random (MNAR) suggests that missingness remains dependent upon the unseen values even when the observed values are considered.

Each type of missingness affects what statistical methodologies should be employed to produce reliable estimates. Under MCAR, listwise deletion is unbiased, but reduces the sample size and usually the efficiency of the study. When MAR exists, both multiple imputation and maximum likelihood estimation allow for valid inference under an appropriately constructed model. MNAR models require sensitivity analysis because observed data alone cannot establish assumptions about unobserved values \parencite{rubin1987multiple}.

This understanding is especially important within clinical neuropsychology. Consider someone with severe attentional impairment who lacks the energy to complete an executive-function task, or a disoriented patient who is not given a memory test. In both examples, the missing value may be related to the value that would exist if the full test had been administered. Therefore, MAR is merely assumed as opposed to being definitively proven. MNAR patterns can coexist with missingness caused by assessment protocols. Although observed data alone cannot identify the missingness mechanism, auxiliary variables can make MAR more plausible by incorporating clinically relevant information \parencite{little2019statistical}.

The amount of missing data alone cannot establish the risk of bias. Instead, it is the reason(s) for which the data are missing along with observed associations among other measures that influence the quality of imputed values. To provide sufficient evidence for conditional estimation, variables should be selected to include in the analysis such that there exists enough observed data on each variable. Subsequent cluster analyses should examine how imputation decisions affect the structure recovered.

\section{Imputation Methods}\label{sec:lit_imputation}
The ten methods presented in this study are ordered by increasing complexity. Let the data matrix $X \in \mathbb{R}^{n \times p}$ contain \(n\) assessments and \(p\) variables. For assessment \(i\), $x_{ij}$ denotes the value of variable \(j\). Let $\Omega = \{(i, j): x_{ij} \text{ is observed}\}$. Then $\Omega_j = \{i : (i, j) \in \Omega\}$ represents the observed rows for the \(j\)-th variable. Define the operator $P_\Omega$ as follows: for any entry $x_{ij}$ of the matrix $X$, $P_\Omega(x_{ij}) = x_{ij}$, if $(i,j) \in \Omega$; otherwise, $P_\Omega(x_{ij}) = 0$. The task is to estimate the entries outside $\Omega$, with estimates denoted by $\hat{x}_{ij}$.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Mean Imputation Method}.\par\nobreak

Mean imputation calculates the average of the observed values of a variable and assigns that mean to each missing value of the same variable.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mean}
\hat{x}_{ij}=\bar{x}_j=\frac{1}{|\Omega_j|}\sum_{i\in\Omega_j}x_{ij}.
\end{equation}

Mean imputation is extremely rapid and is non-iterative. However, Mean imputation reduces variance and covariances among variables, alters marginal distribution of variables and decreases estimated standard error of parameters. Therefore, Mean imputation was employed exclusively as a reference point or "baseline" for purposes of this investigation.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{K-Nearest Neighbors (KNN)}.\par\nobreak

For each missing value, KNN imputation finds \(k\) nearest "donor cases" based upon Euclidean distance to define the degree of similarity. Then, KNN imputes a weighted average of values from these donor-cases into the missing values. The weights are inversely proportional to the distances, as follows:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:knn}
\hat{x}_{ij}=\frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_i}w_{ik}\,x_{kj}}{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}_i}w_{ik}},\qquad w_{ik}=\frac{1}{d(i,k)},
\end{equation}

where \(\mathcal{N}_i\) is the set of \(k\)-nearest neighbors and \(d(i,k)\) is the distance between observations in the jointly observed features. KNN is non-parametric and can capture local data structure more effectively than mean imputation. When using KNN as an imputation method you need to be careful when you are choosing your \(k\). If you choose too low of a \(k\), you may be fitting the noise instead of the signal. If you select too large of a \(k\), you may "smooth" away important patterns in your data. Larger datasets require longer computation times, which is a consideration for larger datasets.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations (MICE)}.\par\nobreak

MICE (Multiple Imputation by Chained Equations) is used for imputation of each variable in relation to all other variables in the dataset. However, each variable is iteratively imputed, over multiple iterations \parencite{vanbuuren2011mice}. Each iteration \(t\) draws new values for each variable from its conditional distribution:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mice}
x_{ij}^{(t)}\sim p\!\left(x_{ij}\,\middle|\,\mathbf{x}_{i,-j}^{(t)};\,\theta_j\right),\qquad j=1,\dots,p,
\end{equation}

Depending on how you want to represent your variable(s), you may define different types of conditional models; i.e., linear models for continuous variables or logistic models for binary variables. The advantage of MICE is that it can maintain relationships between multiple variables and generate multiple imputations to allow you to measure uncertainty. Some disadvantages include defining a model for every variable and possible convergence problems.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Predictive Mean Matching (PMM)}.\par\nobreak

PMM (Predictive Mean Matching) is commonly used in conjunction with MICE (i.e., as a semi-parametric imputation method). First, a regression model is created to predict values. Then, a missing value is replaced by a random selection of an observed donor value with a comparable model prediction:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pmm}
\hat{x}_{ij}\sim\operatorname{Unif}\bigl\{x_{kj}:k\in\mathcal{D}_{ij}\bigr\},\qquad
\mathcal{D}_{ij}=\bigl\{\,d\text{ observed }k\text{ minimising }|\hat{x}_{kj}-\hat{x}_{ij}|\,\bigr\}.
\end{equation}

Predictive mean matching selects observed donor values, keeping imputations within the observed range. This avoids impossible values, such as a negative score on a test with a floor. A limited donor pool can lead to repeated use of the same values.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{MissForest}.\par\nobreak

MissForest utilizes iterative random forests for its missing value imputation algorithm \parencite{stekhoven2012missforest}. For the first step, for every variable \(j\), a forest \(f_j\) is created based on all instances of \(j\) where \(j\) is known. All other variables are treated as predictor variables. Next, a missing value is replaced by using the newly constructed forest \(f_j\). The process repeats until changes are small.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:missforest}
\hat{x}_{ij}=f_j\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{i,-j}\right)\quad\text{until}\quad \sum_{(i,j)\notin\Omega}\bigl(x_{ij}^{(t)}-x_{ij}^{(t-1)}\bigr)^2 \text{ stops decreasing.}
\end{equation}

Since MissForest does not assume parametric distributions, it allows the discovery of complex relationships between variables and can also be applied to either continuous or categorical variables. Generally, MissForest often performs well on benchmark datasets. A significant drawback of MissForest is that it can be quite time consuming and its computational efficiency decreases rapidly with increasing amounts of data or number of trees.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Expectation-Maximization (EM)}.\par\nobreak

Another iterative method is expectation-maximization (EM) \parencite{dempster1977maximum}. EM works by alternating between two different types of steps: An E-step which estimates the expected sufficient statistic, and an M-step which maximizes the likelihood function. Under a multivariate normal model, the iterations estimate model parameters and missing blocks can be filled using their conditional means:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:em}
\hat{\mathbf{x}}_{\mathrm{mis}}=\boldsymbol\mu_{\mathrm{mis}}+\Sigma_{\mathrm{mis},\mathrm{obs}}\,\Sigma_{\mathrm{obs},\mathrm{obs}}^{-1}\!\left(\mathbf{x}_{\mathrm{obs}}-\boldsymbol\mu_{\mathrm{obs}}\right),
\end{equation}

where \(\boldsymbol\mu\) and \(\Sigma\) are recomputed after every iteration. We should note that assuming a multivariate normal distribution may be unrealistic when there are many highly skewed or bounded variables.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{SoftImpute}.\par\nobreak

SoftImpute uses Singular Value Decomposition (SVD) with soft-thresholding to create a low-rank approximation of the original input matrix \parencite{mazumder2010spectral}. Specifically SoftImpute solves:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:softimpute}
\min_{Z}\ \tfrac12\bigl\lVert P_\Omega(\mathbf{X}-Z)\bigr\rVert_F^2+\lambda\lVert Z\rVert_*,
\end{equation}

where \(S_\lambda(Z)=U\operatorname{diag}\bigl((d_i-\lambda)_+\bigr)V^\top\). \(\lambda\) determines how much to shrink the singular values and therefore the rank of the estimated matrix. While SoftImpute can work efficiently on very large sparse matrices, it does not necessarily capture structure in the data that cannot be captured through a low rank estimate.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NMF)}.\par\nobreak

Like SoftImpute, Non-Negative Matrix Factorization (NNMF), or NMF, also uses lower dimensional representations of the data. NMF creates two non-negative matrices \parencite{lee1999learning} that together represent the original input matrix:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:nmf}
\min_{W\ge0,\,H\ge0}\ \bigl\lVert P_\Omega(\mathbf{X}-WH)\bigr\rVert_F^2,\qquad W\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times r}_{\ge0},\ H\in\mathbb{R}^{r\times p}_{\ge0}.
\end{equation}

NMF preserves the constraint when test scores are non-negative. The factors produced by NNMF provide an additive way of representing the score matrix. Sometimes, the factors of the score matrix can have meaningful interpretations. However, like all other methods described here, finding a good number of factors (i.e. determining a suitable \(r\)) is critical for producing useful results.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Denoising Autoencoder (DAE)}.\par\nobreak

Within the MIDA paradigm, Denoising AutoEncoders (DAE's) learn a Neural Network to take in corrupt versions of the data and output reconstructions of the data \parencite{gondara2018mida}. For an encoder $f_\theta$, decoder $g_\theta$ and corrupted version of the data $\tilde{\mathbf{x}}$ , they try to find the minimum distance between their reconstruction of the original input and the original input:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dae}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{DAE}}(\theta)=\mathbb{E}\bigl\lVert\mathbf{x}-g_\theta\!\left(f_\theta(\tilde{\mathbf{x}})\right)\bigr\rVert^2.
\end{equation}

The resulting imputed values are determined by averaging the values generated by multiple neural-network models, each initialized with a different random seed. Denoising autoencoders may outperform traditional imputation techniques when the relationships among variables are nonlinear.

\par\medskip
\noindent\textbf{Variational Autoencoder (VAE)}.\par\nobreak

For example, VAEs use probabilistic generative models to define probability distributions in latent variable space. In so doing, observations are represented as distributions over latent variables \parencite{kingma2014auto, mccoy2018variational}. The evidence lower bound (ELBO) can be maximized by:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:vae}
\mathcal{L}_{\mathrm{VAE}}=\mathbb{E}_{q_\phi(z\mid x)}\!\left[\log p_\theta(x\mid z)\right]-\beta\,D_{\mathrm{KL}}\!\left(q_\phi(z\mid x)\,\big\Vert\,p(z)\right),
\end{equation}

using a standard normal prior $p(z)=\mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I})$. In related work, \parencite{mattei2019miwae} used importance-weighted bounds for missing data, whereas \parencite{yoon2018gain} proposed a GAN-based method. Using VAEs provides an inherent way of producing imputed values based on samples from the latent distribution, followed by decoding each sample. If trained properly this also allows for a representation of the uncertainty of each individual value.

Deep learning techniques have advantages and disadvantages. They may take more computation time than simpler methods, but can learn nonlinear relationships without a manually specified simple functional form for each variable. Many architectures also contain stochastic elements: dropout in DAEs and latent sampling in VAEs are examples. These elements can be used to generate multiple imputations. However, deep learning models can overfit and their performance often depends heavily on architectural choices. This dissertation examines how these methods function on the small to medium-sized, relatively low-dimensional datasets typical of clinical neuropsychology \parencite{prakash2024benchmarking}.

\parencite{afkanpour2024identify} divided imputation methods for clinical research into four categories. I add a fifth for the deep learning methods used here. Categories one through three are comprised of the well-known methodologies which include: Category One: Traditional Methods (mean imputation, EM, MICE); Category Two: Machine Learning Methods (KNN, MissForest); Category Three: Hybrid Methods (PMM). Matrix Completion represents Category Four (SoftImpute and NMF). Category Five was created specifically for Deep Learning Methodologies utilized in our study (DAEs and VAEs). Utilizing these five categories will allow us to determine whether the methodologies in each category provide consistent recovery of cognitive structures when utilizing each methodology with varying assumptions about estimating missing data.

\section{Clustering in Neuropsychology}
Clustering is used as a statistical technique in neuropsychological research. Clinical judgments about diagnosis or disability after stroke, traumatic brain injury (TBI), or in mild cognitive impairment (MCI) often rely on categorical assessments. Across many different types of clinical populations (e.g. TBI; MCI), cluster analysis has been used. Many of the resultant analyses have indicated that the statistically derived groups of performance within the realm of cognition do not consistently match the clinical diagnosis or severity category that are given to those same populations. Therefore, from an empirical perspective, it appears that when clinicians utilize classification systems to describe cognitive variability among individuals post-injury, they do not fully capture the full range of cognitive variability present.

A literature review of data-driven cognitive phenotyping after brain injury was conducted by \parencite{garcia2020data}. Trends were consistent in their reviews. First, many of the studies found two to four statistically defined cognitive clusters. Second, many of the studies found clusters based upon whether certain cognitive domains were more functional than others relative to an individual's overall level of functioning. Third, when possible and relevant, researchers found that group membership was significantly related to clinically important outcomes (i.e. return to work).

Many aspects of research on data-driven cognitive phenotyping following brain injury are still underdeveloped. Many prior studies have been based on samples that ranged from approximately 100 to less than 200 subjects. The common practice among researchers was to exclude missing values by using complete cases. When missingness is MCAR (completely at random) this approach may be suitable for determining the characteristics of the remaining sample; however, when there is some degree of information within the missingness, then the use of complete cases can bias the resulting sample. Very few studies have investigated how sensitive clustering algorithms are to various parameter choices.

Therefore, this dissertation employed four distinct approaches to reduce the impact of these limitations. In Chapter \ref{Chapter3} I used a much larger clinical database; compared ten imputation strategies to each other and to complete case analyses; examined sensitivity to hyperparameters and to several missingness thresholds; and tested HDBSCAN as an alternative method to \(k\)-means. Unlike \(k\)-means, HDBSCAN does not require the number of clusters \(k\) to be specified before application. Instead HDBSCAN identifies clusters where the density of data points exceeds certain threshold(s); data points which do not fit into a cluster are identified as "noise" or outliers.

\section{UMAP and HDBSCAN}
The clustering process is composed of two key components: the UMAP for reducing the dimensionality of the input dataset and identifying clusters using HDBSCAN.

\subsection{UMAP}
The first part (UMAP) applies a type of non-linear manifold learning. It seeks to reduce high-dimensional datasets to lower-dimensional representations while retaining as much of their original structure as possible. Because PCA and other forms of linear reductions cannot capture non-linear relationships present in the original data, UMAP can. As such, there are trade-offs involved with preserving the overall topological features of the data (i.e., globally) versus the local details, so we use three parameters to find a suitable balance between global and local structure:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \texttt{n\_neighbors}: This determines the number of neighboring points in the original data that should be used in the construction of each point's neighborhood graph. The larger this value is, the broader your view will be of the global properties of the data. Conversely, if you choose smaller values, you will have better insight into local relationships.

    \item \texttt{min\_dist}: This governs how closely points can be placed together in the reduced space. If you increase this value, it results in spreading points farther apart from one another; conversely, setting it to a smaller value allows for denser grouping of related points.

    \item \texttt{n\_components}: This is the target number of dimensions in the reduced space.

\end{itemize}

UMAP creates a weighted graph of neighboring objects. Each connection has a directed membership probability \(p_{j\mid i}\) linking an object \(x_i\) to one of its neighbors \(x_j\), as defined in Equation \ref{eq:umap_p}. Two parameters enter this calculation: \(\rho_i\), the distance from \(x_i\) to its closest neighbor, and \(\sigma_i\), a calibration factor chosen so that \(\sum_j p_{j\mid i}=\log_2(k)\), with \(k\) specified by \texttt{n\_neighbors}. The directed probabilities are made symmetric using Equation \ref{eq:umap_sym}. In Equation \ref{eq:umap_q}, \(q_{ij}\) is the low-dimensional membership probability, with \(a\) and \(b\) determined by \texttt{min\_dist}. Lastly, UMAP minimizes the cross-entropy given in Equation \ref{eq:umap_ce} between the high-dimensional and low-dimensional memberships.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:umap_p}
p_{j\mid i}=\exp\!\left(-\frac{\max\{0,\,d(x_i,x_j)-\rho_i\}}{\sigma_i}\right),
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:umap_sym}
p_{ij}=p_{j\mid i}+p_{i\mid j}-p_{j\mid i}p_{i\mid j}.
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:umap_q}
q_{ij}=\left(1+a\,\lVert y_i-y_j\rVert_2^{2b}\right)^{-1},
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:umap_ce}
C=\sum_{i\ne j}\left[p_{ij}\log\frac{p_{ij}}{q_{ij}}+(1-p_{ij})\log\frac{1-p_{ij}}{1-q_{ij}}\right],
\end{equation}

\subsection{HDBSCAN}
HDBSCAN represents data through a hierarchy of clusters at varying levels of density and identifies groupings that persist across that hierarchy. With respect to the recovery of variable-density and nested clusters, DBSCAN suffers in comparison because it uses a constant radius. HDBSCAN's explicit density hierarchy addresses this limitation. It selects stable clusters without requiring their number in advance and assigns the remaining observations to noise. Three key parameters govern its operation:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \texttt{min\_samples} - Density estimation parameter.

    \item \texttt{min\_cluster\_size} - Minimum size of a valid cluster.

    \item \texttt{cluster\_selection\_epsilon} - Distance threshold used to merge nearby clusters.

\end{itemize}

To connect observations, HDBSCAN uses mutual reachability (Equation \ref{eq:mreach}), which adjusts distances to reflect local density. A minimum spanning tree over those distances provides the basis for the hierarchy. Successive levels form groups of observations, and cluster persistence is assessed through stability (Equation \ref{eq:hdbscan_stab}). For a given cluster \(C\), \(\lambda_{\mathrm{birth}}(C)\) is the density level at which \(C\) first appeared; \(\lambda_{\max}(x,C)\) is the highest density level at which \(x\) belonged to \(C\). Observations outside the selected persistent clusters are assigned to noise.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:mreach}
d_{\mathrm{mreach}}(a,b)=\max\bigl\{\operatorname{core}_k(a),\,\operatorname{core}_k(b),\,d(a,b)\bigr\}.
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}\label{eq:hdbscan_stab}
S(C)=\sum_{x\in C}\bigl(\lambda_{\max}(x,C)-\lambda_{\mathrm{birth}}(C)\bigr),\qquad \lambda=\frac{1}{d_{\mathrm{mreach}}},
\end{equation}

The ability to extract structure from high-dimensional datasets has led to applications of UMAP and HDBSCAN in computational biology and related fields.

\section{Cluster Validation}
The current research uses three different techniques in order to evaluate if the cluster structures produced by these methods are appropriate since they have no gold standard for reference. Internal validation methods, external consistency measures, as well as sensitivity analysis are used to indirectly assess the validity of this research.

\subsection{Internal Validation}
Internal validation involves several aspects of cluster analysis. In this research we have used silhouette measures \parencite{rousseeuw1987silhouettes} to provide comparison of how well points in a cluster are cohesive (i.e., similar), with respect to how separated they are from points in their nearest neighbor cluster(s). A silhouette value will be a number in [-1,+1]. Silhouette scores greater than 0 indicate that a point is closer, on average, to other points assigned to the same cluster than to any other cluster. Scores close to 0 indicate little difference among those alternatives; while negative scores indicate that the point is actually closer to another cluster. These values do not determine whether an assignment is clinically correct. Each silhouette measure is calculated as per Equation \ref{eq:silhouette}, and is composed of two components: \(a(i)\); the mean distance between each point and every other point in the same cluster; and \(b(i)\); the mean distance from the point to every member of the nearest alternative cluster. The overall average silhouette for a set of clusters can be found by calculating the average of all \(s(i)\) values and will serve as our primary method of internally evaluating the quality of the clusters generated. Noise percentage is also reported. It is the proportion of observations left unassigned by density clustering and therefore describes its coverage.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:silhouette}
s(i)=\frac{b(i)-a(i)}{\max\{a(i),\,b(i)\}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{External Validation}
Comparing cluster models for the same observations (e.g. cluster assignments generated using different imputation techniques), can be achieved by evaluating the degree of concordance among the resultant cluster models. An effective way to perform this evaluation is through the application of the adjusted Rand index (ARI) \parencite{hubert1985comparing}. This metric evaluates the degree of concordance between the pair-wise cluster assignments to evaluate if the concordance exceeds chance.

To utilize the formula shown in Equation \ref{eq:ari}, we must first identify two clusterings of \(n\) objects denoted as $U = \{U_i\}$ and $V = \{V_j\}$. We will also need to identify contingency counts denoted as $n_{ij} = |U_i \cap V_j|$, where i and j index the clusters in U and V. The marginal totals denoted as $a_i = \sum_j n_{ij}$ represent the number of elements in \(U_i\). The marginal totals denoted as $b_j = \sum_i n_{ij}$ represent the number of elements in \(V_j\).

\begin{equation}\label{eq:ari}
\mathrm{ARI}=\frac{\displaystyle\sum_{ij}\binom{n_{ij}}{2}-\Bigl[\sum_i\binom{a_i}{2}\sum_j\binom{b_j}{2}\Bigr]\Big/\binom{n}{2}}{\displaystyle\tfrac12\Bigl[\sum_i\binom{a_i}{2}+\sum_j\binom{b_j}{2}\Bigr]-\Bigl[\sum_i\binom{a_i}{2}\sum_j\binom{b_j}{2}\Bigr]\Big/\binom{n}{2}}.
\end{equation}

When ARI is maximally positive (+1) there exists a complete concordance between both cluster models. Conversely, ARI values which equal 0 indicate that the concordance observed between both cluster models equates to a chance baseline. Finally, negative values of ARI will indicate less than expected concordance between both cluster models \parencite{robert2020comparing}.

In addition to the usage of ARI I also make use of normalized mutual information (NMI). NMI quantifies how much one cluster model provides information about another clustering, while providing normalization with respect to the entropy of each cluster model label.

The results of comparing the cluster assignment solutions through mutual information (i.e., $I(U;V)$) and Shannon entropy (i.e., \(H(\cdot)\)) \parencite{shannon1948mathematical} as expressed in Equation \ref{eq:nmi} can range from 1 when there is perfect agreement among cluster assignments to 0 when they are found to be statistically independent.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:nmi}
\mathrm{NMI}(U,V)=\frac{2\,I(U;V)}{H(U)+H(V)},
\end{equation}

To measure the degree that clusters in one solution are either similar or dissimilar to those in another solution, I utilize the Jaccard Index. As indicated in Equation \ref{eq:jaccard}, where \(A\) and \(B\) represent the sets of observations that comprise the clusters being evaluated for similarity, the Jaccard Index represents the size of the intersection of the sets divided by the size of the union of the sets, with values in [0, 1]. Therefore, larger values of the index would indicate that the cluster memberships are more similar.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:jaccard}
J(A,B)=\frac{|A\cap B|}{|A\cup B|}.
\end{equation}

In addition to evaluating the similarity/dissimilarity between clusters across two solutions, I utilize clusterwise stability matching \parencite{hennig2007cluster}, to evaluate whether cluster assignments remain stable after repeated sampling. In each of \(R\) resampling iterations, I pair an original cluster \(C_k\) with the resampled cluster having the greatest Jaccard overlap with it. I then average these maximum overlaps across the iterations. This average value is referred to as the "stability score" for \(C_k\) and is expressed as in Equation \ref{eq:hennig}. A per-cluster score > 0.7 is used here as the stability criterion.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:hennig}
\hat{S}_k=\frac{1}{R}\sum_{r=1}^{R}\max_{k'}\,J\!\left(C_k,\,C_{k'}^{(r)}\right).
\end{equation}

\enlargethispage{2\baselineskip}
Taken together, NMI and ARI enable a quantitative comparison of similarities and differences between two cluster assignments. Although both metrics enable an evaluation of agreement between two cluster assignments they are metrics that measure agreement and should not be used as statistical tests of significance.
