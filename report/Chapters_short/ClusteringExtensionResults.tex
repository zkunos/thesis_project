\section{Patient-Level Clustering Extension: Results}\label{sec:extension_results}

The new analyses identified repeatable groupings in cognitive test scores, but their interpretation as distinct clinical phenotypes remains unresolved. These results reflect the earliest eligible assessment for each of the 7,285 patients, which included 5,828 patients who were part of the discovery cohort and 1,457 patients from the validation cohort. These assessments cannot be used as a direct comparison in terms of performance when compared to the original assessment-level UMAP analysis since these two cohorts have differences in terms of composition, representation and validation strategy.

\subsection{Original-Space Comparisons and Cluster Count}

Representative solutions generated via discovery data analysis are summarized in Table \ref{tab:extension_benchmarks}. It appears that for the reference six-domain representation, two-group k-means and PAM (Partitioning Around Medoids) algorithms were best suited. When applied directly to both of the tested minimum cluster sizes, however, HDBSCAN provided only "noise" classifications; UMAP + HDBSCAN did produce group assignments. In fact, increasing the size of the neighborhood in UMAP (i.e., changing the number from 15 to 50) resulted in changes in the number of groups identified as well as lessened separation among the groups in terms of the measured domains. As such, these results suggest that examining whether or not the measured feature representations provide adequate support for the partitions identified through the embedded representations may be useful. Nonetheless, it seems reasonable to assume additional research will need to be conducted into identifying different methods for defining the parameters of the density models.

The k-means algorithm chose a consistent number of groups (two) in each of the twenty stochastic imputations made during the discovery-silhouette search. This was a consistent choice among $k=2,\ldots,8$, not a test against one population. The eight-component full Gaussian domain model also reached its maximum number of components. Neither of the other methods examined (minimum BIC at the boundary, silhouette-based selection) appeared to identify a single "natural" number of clusters.

\subsection{Independent Imputation and Patient Stability}

The two-, and three-cluster K-Means analysis had very good consistency under the constrained Bayesian imputation model, and also when using the "robust domain" representations as shown in Table \ref{tab:extension_stability}. The average ARI among the pairs of clusters generated from the fixed 3-cluster patient sub-samples was 0.968. When the clustering seed was changed while holding the input constant the average ARI was 1.000. The average Jaccard match value over the three cluster assignments was 0.994, 0.959 and 0.947 respectively. These numbers demonstrate repeatability of the grouping across this preprocessing pipeline and do not indicate that there is a clinically meaningful distinction between these groupings.

The Consensus Agreement based on Average Linkage and Greedy mean NMI for both K-Means analyses at $k = 2$ and $k = 3$ was nearly identical. However, the generic NMF Ensemble Aggregation Method performed differently and was much less consistent than the other two methods: For example, a 2 Cluster K-Means Ensemble failed to converge after reaching an Iteration Limit, and collapsed into one cluster assignment. Similarly, the generic NMF Ensemble Aggregation Method used for the 3 Cluster K-Means Ensemble produced only two clusters. As such, this result relates only to the results of Ensemble Aggregation Methods, and should not be generalized to reflect performance of all possible implementations of the NMF Imputation Model.

\subsection{Scaling, Missingness and Preprocessing Sensitivity}

With robust scaling, discovery variances were 7.93 for language and 5.76 for orientation, compared with 0.44 for memory. Additionally, the first principal component of the data in the robust domain explained 63.3\% of the total variability in the data and it was heavily loaded on both the language and the orientation dimensions. Narrow central ranges of ceiling-heavy scores amplify tails under interquartile-range scaling. With equal-domain scaling, the first principal component explained 58.5\% of variance. We also saw substantial discrepancies between the three-group validation assignments made using the two types of scaling. In fact, we estimated an adjusted rand index (ARI) of .243 between the two sets of three-group validation assignments.

The average silhouette value for robust three-group labels was .608 in robust space and .440 in equal-domain space. In contrast, the average silhouette values for equal-domain labels were .172 and .283 for robust and equal-domain spaces respectively. Figure \ref{fig:extension_scaling} separates the contributions from partitioning from those due to measurement geometry, neither geometry having been clinically validated. By comparison, application of equal-domain scaling resulted in lower average agreement levels among all possible combinations of imputed labels for two-group K-Means (.769), and for three-group K-Means (.747).

Robust three-group validation labels gave groups of 1221 patients, 185 patients, and 51 patients. The smallest group displayed clear deficits in terms of language abilities. Equal-domain scaling gave groups of 194 patients, 571 patients, and 692 patients with varying profiles compared to one another (see Figure \ref{fig:extension_profiles}). There is no empirical support here for transferring historical clinical tier names to the new labels.

There were also relations between the availability of testing and the fixed cluster allocations. Cramér's $V$ for cluster allocation versus missingness was 0.412 for \texttt{MRAVLT075}, and 0.371 for \texttt{FEPMR}. Both tests had substantial variation in missingness across clusters, ranging from 10.2--80.4\%, and 16.1--86.3\%. Although this suggests some type of relation between cluster allocations and missingness, it does not provide enough evidence to suggest that missingness is what caused the clustering of the data. However, because of the variability present we cannot treat the reliability estimates generated using just one of the imputation techniques as evidence of no effect due to test administration.

The mean-imputed data set at $k=3$ had an Adjusted Rand Index (ARI) of .921 compared to the reference grouping, while the k-nearest neighbors (KNN) imputed data set at $k=3$ had an ARI of .538 as compared to the reference groupings. When we required at least seven observed tests per patient, 5,263 discovery and 1,339 validation patients remained. The ARI on common validation patients was 0.903. After de-clipping, we had an ARI of .958. Deterministically imputing with a 50 iteration limit, we achieved convergence by iteration \#37, and had no label changes (ARI = 1.00). Using the "frozen" discovery transformation to cluster the validation patients independently, we obtained an ARI of .895. This statistic measures agreement between partitions; it is not another measure of predictive accuracy.

Comparing the results from the unaligned raw-test aggregation to those from the directionally aligned standardized-domain for the three-group partition, we found ARIs of .703 for all assessments, and .629 for complete-cases. There was also a high negative correlation ($-0.749$) between our two constructed attention-composite variables, so we suspect the scale/direction alignment affected these statistics. Thus, they are best viewed as sensitivity analyses using completed values as constants, and should not be considered repeats of our previously presented supplemental multiple imputation and complete-case analyses.

\subsection{Incomplete-Data Mixtures and Continuous References}

Of the 32 incomplete-data mixture fits, 28 achieved numerical convergence (Table \ref{tab:extension_mixtures}). Furthermore, among all those which fulfilled both size and convergence criteria the $t$ family with four degrees of freedom had the smallest BIC and highest validation likelihood. Both $t$-families selected eight components at the upper search bound, leaving the optimal component count unresolved. Their six-domain silhouettes were small. Likewise, the ICL identified eight components for the $t$ families but only six rather than seven for the contaminated Gaussian.

Although a better fit of a distribution to the data does not provide conclusive evidence regarding identifying eight distinct clinical phenotypes from the selected data it is possible that the multiple components identified represent either discrete score masses, skewness, and/or the tails of distributions.

Posterior probabilities quantify membership uncertainty conditional on the selected family and component count; they do not account for family-selection uncertainty or the missingness process.

As indicated above, the maximum k-means silhouette obtained in our 1000 patient continuous reference screen was 0.676 and the fitted-reference tail fraction was 0.0099. The PC1 dip-statistic tail fraction was 0.9901. We identified two clear clusters by the positive control using a silhouette measure of 0.917. As such, we have demonstrated using silhouette measures alone that we have surpassed at least one particular copula reference; however, we find no indication of excessive multimodality when comparing our projection statistics against this reference. These descriptive reference comparisons do not constitute an omnibus test establishing clinical phenotypes or excluding every continuous explanation.

\subsection{Longitudinal Findings and Overall Interpretation}

The total number of patients in the eligible trajectory cohort was 2,013. Of these, 1,590 belonged to the discovery group and 423 to the validation group. Convergence occurred for all of the four fitted class count combinations (Table \ref{tab:extension_trajectories}). However, adding classes improved BIC and validation likelihood. At least one subgroup in the models with multiple classes had an expected membership below five percent. Because of this, we applied the rule to force the selection of one continuous patient random effect trajectory class. Multiclass resampling was not triggered. As such, it cannot be concluded that all patients recovered uniformly. The nonlinear variation and presence of very small subgroups still have to be resolved.

In addition to providing further support for the idea that the same cognitive assessment could be used to replicate similar partitions, this study's ability to generalize beyond its own findings is somewhat limited because replicating results is contingent upon how the participants' data are represented and imputed. Specifically, there are several ways in which participant representation can vary. For example, how participants are scaled into groups, as well as what level of missingness exists within their data, will influence whether participants are assigned to groups. Also, the choice of assumed distributional families for the observed variables will impact both the number of components or clusters found and how those components/clusters are defined. Further, our results indicate that distinct clinical phenotypes were not established. The lack of availability of an independent functional outcome measure limits testing for clinical utility.
