\section{Cognitive Structure and the Limits of Phenotype Claims}
The historical assessment showed a broad cognitive-severity structure among individuals with acquired brain injury (ABI). Patient-level extensions showed consistent score groupings that varied based upon scale selection and imputation. These assessments, individually and collectively, did not provide evidence for distinct clinical phenotypes; conversely, there was no direct evidence suggesting that clinical phenotypes may be non-existent. To classify historical assessments in the "above-average", "near-normal", or "global-impairment" tiers, I looked at performance across the whole battery. While each tier has different levels of performance, each does not represent a unique shape of performance. All six areas of the assessments produced similar patterns of movement. As can be observed in Figure \ref{fig:radar_profiles}, the first principal component explained approximately 56\% of the total variability in domain scores. Additionally, it loaded positively on every single one of the six areas at virtually equivalent magnitude. Based upon this information, I interpret the results as showing a pattern of severity-based stratification, as opposed to identifying specific cognitive patterns. Furthermore, the tiers were associated with clinical unit assignment (see \textsection{}\ref{sec:h3_results}), but this analysis did not show additional predictive value beyond the broader diagnostic grouping.

In addition to the findings demonstrating the extent to which variation in neuropsychological test performance reflects variation associated with the domain tested versus a general factor, the findings further illustrate the size of the proportion of the recoverable structure within this routine data collection cohort that is accounted for by a general severity dimension. While secondary variations related to domain specificity were observed, I found no evidence to indicate that they would be responsible for determining clusters. Therefore, based upon the findings of these analyses, there exists empirical support for both clinical experience (i.e., global impairment patterns as a method of separating patients prior to identifying dissociative patterns) \parencite{maas2017traumatic}, and the positive manifold of inter-test correlations commonly reported in neuropsychological studies \parencite{lezak2012neuropsychological}. The results of the phenotypic analysis presented herein also indicate that the findings represent a somewhat conservative estimate of the phenomenon being studied when contrasted with those of previous examinations. For example, even after applying robustness procedures (i.e., imputation-based sensitivity testing) overall severity emerged as a defining characteristic of subgroups before profile pattern characteristics were identified. Given that domain-based aggregation and density-based clustering occurs along manifolds that are characterized by the presence of a general factor accounting for most of the variance among tests, I do not attach significant importance to the absence of evidence indicating narrow dissociations, including attention-specific subgroups, which may exist but be undetectable due to their small magnitude or orthogonal nature relative to the primary severity gradient.

While this current research does not conflict with previous studies on the variety of dissociable cognitive profiles that arise from brain injury \parencite{garcia2020data} in terms of whether or not such profiles do exist, the results provide a qualification of how these prior findings should be interpreted. The methodology used by García-Rudolph et al. differs from mine in several ways. First, the methodology I employed (UMAP + HDBSCAN) allows identification of irregular shapes and/or "noise" at low densities. Second, I used assessment resampling to assess cluster reproduction. Finally, I standardize, winsorize, and align all of my variable measures in a specific direction before creating composite variables. Each preprocessing decision is significant -- especially given large variance in raw score measurements (such as subject's completion time for Trail Making Tests), creation of artificial axes due to non-cognitive data, or large amounts of missing data where the density estimates may be skewed. Therefore apparent differences in phenotypes due to pre-processing decisions may not represent real structure differences in neuropsychology. This is discussed further in Chapter \ref{Chapter6}.

The first hypothesis was only partially supported (Section \ref{sec:h1_results}). All ten imputation methodologies produced adequate levels of both silhouette and noise metric assessments. In other words, all ten of the imputation methodologies recovered ordered bands of severity. However, while overall core Jaccard varied across the ten methodologies from 0.509 to 0.630, individual matched-cluster comparisons varied from 0.383 to 0.633 and cluster identifications were identified between three and five. These results support ordered severity bands, but do not establish sharply defined, uniformly reproducible phenotypes. Phenotype boundaries can shift without disrupting the underlying severity banding order.

I suggest that the three-tiered model produced from the original MICE pipeline represent an interpretable discrete representation of a continuum. Therefore, it cannot be considered as empirical evidence of natural kinds. Tier numbering was based on configuration and typically ranged from three to five in terms of number of tiers, when all configurations are compared. The cluster count varied depending on both the imputation analysis and the threshold sensitivity analyses; however, the ordering according to degree of cognitive impairment was consistent across all analyses. A continuous summary measure or clearly labeled descriptive categories warrant additional validation. However, at this time, there has been no evaluation of the relative clinical utility of these two alternatives.

\section{Imputation Sensitivity Under a Fixed Partition}
I believe Hypothesis 2 represents my biggest contribution to methodology but in a very focused way. First, I applied both UMAP and HDBSCAN for identification of the primary clusters. Second, I performed a separate fixed-reference diagnostic test by performing one \(k\)-means fit to the MICE embedding and then projecting all of the other imputation data onto this fixed structure. In the diagnostic (see \textsection{}\ref{sec:h2_results}), the average ARI value was above 0.70 among the 45 method pairings and each of the 45 had an ARI greater than 0.50. The groupings were generally consistent across the imputed data in that fixed structure. In fact, I think the ability to identify the groupings is indicative of a strong severity gradient while multidimensional profile structures are harder to find and may be poorly defined or at least fragile. The two aspects of the grouping analysis could contribute to its robustness while allowing for variability in methods \parencite{rubin1987multiple}.

Although I believe my method for dealing with the imputed data affected how many tiers I found, as well as their quality, I am inclined to think that my findings regarding the position of patients on the cognitive-severity continuum may vary less depending on what kind of imputation technique I employ. Overall, the fixed-reference diagnostic preserved many of the assignments between all compared imputations. The claim does not hold up when we examine independent model fits: the new KNN sensitivity yields an ARI of 0.538 against the reference three-group partition. That being said, while I do not feel these results indicate that missing data should be ignored, I would say that researchers can develop approaches to address missingness systematically and report on the methodology they use, and clinicians can treat tier labels as resources to support decision-making and judgment, rather than as replacements for educational, training and experiential based practice.

There was an obvious difference in the characteristics of both the smooth, model-based (EM, DAE, VAE) vs. the local/donor-type (KNN) imputation techniques (see Figure \ref{fig:h2_dendrogram}). While there is literature that categorizes various types of imputation methodologies (machine learning/statistical/deep learning), it seems to me that another way to categorize imputation methodologies is to separate them into two families: the "smooth" family of models where imputed values generally trend towards a model-based conditional mean, and local/donor models where estimates for a single assessment are made using a few neighboring assessments (donors).

The moderate association (\(r = 0.47\); \textsection{}\ref{sec:h2_results}) identified by the correlation at the assessment level between the amount of imputed data and total instability suggests that subsequent analysis of the data will require separate consideration of assessments with larger amounts of missing information compared to assessments with relatively small amounts of missing information. This separate consideration may take place through decreased confidence levels of assessments with high percentages of missing data or through multiple imputations being used to generate comparisons.

Consensus clustering offers an accessible way to address this challenge. Ten different imputation strategies were each given a "vote" on individual assessment labels. A consensus cluster vote produced a value representing how much agreement existed regarding assignment of labels to individual assessments based upon the voting results of the different imputation strategies. Across assessments, there was a high degree of average agreement (mean confidence = 0.93); moreover, 86.2\% of assessments had high confidence. I find assessments with low levels of confidence to reflect clinical importance and not just inconvenience. These assessments may represent patients near tier boundaries and should prompt caution or reassessment.

Although MICE is generally accepted to replace missing data prior to clustering on neurocognitive test data, if a researcher anticipates that relationships between neuropsychological assessment variables will likely be nonlinear, MissForest \parencite{stekhoven2012missforest} has been shown to perform well. Due to application of the Holm-Bonferroni adjustment procedure to each of the 45 pairs of comparisons (as described in Section \ref{sec:robustness}), 44 of the 45 pairs rejected the null hypothesis stating that ARI \(\leq 0.50\). The exception was KNN--SoftImpute, whose ARI remained near the threshold. Therefore, within the fixed-reference H2 design, I concluded that there does not appear to be a single strategy for replacing missing data that drives patient placement on the severity dimension. The number of density-based tiers remains more sensitive when UMAP + HDBSCAN is refitted independently.

The amount of sensitivity possibly present in the treatment of missing data is illustrated in Section \ref{sec:robustness}. Comparison of complete case data resulted in differences in the number of clusters identified, where silhouette = 0.257 whereas ARI = 0.625 when comparing MICE-derived solutions to the shared assessments derived through raw test aggregation. While the controlled comparisons in the extension demonstrated some degree of sensitivity related to the directional and scaled alignment, previous list-wise statistical analyses do not represent domain standardized analyses.

\section{Clinical Unit Association}
In order to assess if there is an association between cognitive-severity tier and which clinical unit each participant was assigned to, I used a contingency test on both tier and unit. There was a statistically significant association, with a small effect size according to Cohen's descriptive standards. This association was still present after controlling for the broader diagnostic category that was also provided through the clinical-unit lookup using a stratified sensitivity analysis. Since the broader category and unit details were both retrieved via the same clinical-unit lookup function, I did not view these two sources as providing independent validation.

Although the association may have some clinical implications, it cannot be taken as evidence of predictive capability or causality. When conducting a patient-grouped multinomial sensitivity analysis, when tier was added to the broad diagnostic grouping, held-out log loss decreased from 0.6064 to 0.6051. However, in assessing the patient bootstrap improvement interval for tier, zero was included; and accuracy was identical to three decimal points. Therefore, H3 provides only support for differences in tier distribution among units. Assessing prospective routing or future outcomes requires separate predictors, patient-grouped validation and clinical outcomes collected for that purpose.

\section{Feature Engineering}
Many ways exist to describe cognitive characteristics. Two general approaches use variable-level characteristics and aggregated characteristics. Variable-level characteristics evaluate each individual test by itself. Aggregated characteristics are averages or sums of tests and are designed to represent a specific cognitive domain. In my research, I tested whether an aggregated characteristic of a cognitive domain would have lower multicollinearity than a variable level characteristic. Better conditioning was assessed through lower variance inflation factors (VIF) and a lower condition number.

The results from my data analysis supported H4. My findings did demonstrate lower levels of multicollinearity when using domain-aggregated characteristics instead of variable level characteristics. More specifically, while I found that there were minimal differences in cluster separation (the silhouette value increased only slightly from 0.473 to 0.481), the average VIF decreased from 2.77 to 1.88 and the condition number decreased from 61.7 to 11.8 (see Section \ref{sec:h4_results}). Thus, I believe H4 provides evidence that aggregating domain-test scores can reduce redundancy and create a more interpretable and well-conditioned way of representing assessment data. It should be noted however that this finding does not provide evidence of materially stronger cluster separation. This advantage is also consistent with the practical use of assessing scores on a per-domain basis rather than examining them individually \parencite{lezak2012neuropsychological}.

I wanted to point out that there is a crucial requirement when you are combining test variables at the level of individual variables --- all transformations at the variable level need to occur before the final step of aggregation. The data needs to undergo transformation (winsorization), alignment based on direction, and scale (standardization) before values from each variable can be put together into a single measure of overall performance or a composite. If this order is violated (i.e., if we aggregate values from different variables before standardizing those values), our results will artificially depend upon which variable has the greatest range because of its initial scale.

For instance, if several memory tests were aggregated prior to standardizing them, the final composite is likely to reflect the performance on the test(s) with the largest range rather than whatever aspect of memory it was intended to measure. Similarly, if multiple tests with a high degree of similarity (correlation) enter an unstandardized composite, UMAP and HDBSCAN may utilize the same dimension of cognition to inflate the distance computations.

Aggregating features produces a single feature per domain but does not necessarily ensure that each domain receives equivalent weighting after additional scaling. Additionally, as demonstrated by the patient-level extension, robust scaling provides a larger weight to the tails of both language and orientation.

\section{Feature Importance and What Drives Severity}
The results in \textsection{}\ref{sec:feature_importance} describe relationships among the cognitive domains and the classifications of the random forest surrogate model. Since the random forest surrogate model was 99.4\% accurate in reproducing labels from the same domain scores used to create them, I believe that the ordering generated from the random forest surrogate primarily represents where the random forest surrogate model identified the boundaries of each partition generated during the fitting process rather than the causal reasons underlying each of the partitions. For example, since the random forest surrogate ranked orientation, visuoperceptual ability, and language as being higher than attention and executive function, I don't place much emphasis on the ordering. This is due to high correlations between many of the domains. Additionally, the Gini importance can be greatly influenced depending on whether you have multiple highly correlated predictors \parencite{boulesteix2012overview}.

I place more weight on the principal component analysis (PCA). The PCA indicated that only one principal component accounted for approximately 56 percent of the total variance in the domain scores. Each of the six domains had positive loadings that were all roughly equal in size. Therefore, I believe that this supports a general severity dimension rather than domain-specific dissociation or an attention specific pattern within this particular domain aggregated pipeline. The checks in \textsection{}\ref{sec:robustness} showed considerable assignment variability under assessment subsampling, and tier counts varied with variable-inclusion thresholds. The results demonstrate why researchers conducting data-driven research on phenotyping should explicitly document how their data was pre-processed and provide sensitivity analyses along with providing their original research findings.

\section{Clinical Implications}
I interpret this study's finding as evidence supporting a three-tiered cognitive severity stratification model. However, I see a lot of limitations to using such a model. As I view it clinically, the most significant implication is conservative; they could provide some way of summarizing relative cognitive severity; but they cannot serve as independent clinical decision-making strategies. The medical education and knowledge of local service systems, as well as the professional experience of the clinician responsible for the referral to the rehabilitation services system, will continue to dictate how patients will be placed when they enter the rehabilitation services system. At this time, I do not believe that this research provides enough justification for direct clinical application (beyond carefully interpreting cognitive severity and openly dealing with missing data).

Historically, clinical practitioners have employed visual methods to examine distributions of patient scores in order to identify which patients represent ``outliers'' or ``atypical'' cases. Based upon what was found in this study, I also feel that there continues to be merit in employing algorithms to find clusters and visually examining the distribution of scores in order to identify outliers. Many of the assessments contained in the study had indistinct boundaries among separate groups. As such, I consider the middle tier of cognitive severity to be a part of a continuum with a boundary area cut out from it. Rather than falling into discrete categories of sharp separation, the majority of individuals appear to be distributed across a broad range of cognitive severity. As such, I believe that any future tools developed should focus on identifying assessments that either have difficulty clustering or are close to the boundary between two adjacent areas/clusters rather than attempting to assign each individual assessment a definitive phenotypic category.

Future research on large datasets that include missing cognitive assessment data may benefit from further statistical measures as well. One possible measure would be reporting the percentage of all assessments with an extremely low silhouette value (for example those with a silhouette value less than 0.05) in addition to both overall silhouette values and stability metrics. While this would still require substantive clinical interpretation by a professional, the inclusion of these percentages can provide a quantitative means of identifying the number of assessments located in areas where cluster membership is likely to be ambiguous.

\section{What the Patient-Level Extension Adds}\label{sec:extension_discussion}

I investigated whether the patient groupings could be reproduced. In all twenty imputed datasets, two-group k-means clustering was identified as the most appropriate method to cluster the patients with a mean validation ARI of 0.959. Similarly, using three-group k-means, I found agreement across imputations in the original representation (ARI=0.935), and high agreement under patient subsampling. These analyses have extended the previous fixed-reference H2 diagnostic study by including separate re-fitting of partitions and maintaining the size of the validation set constant. Since the cohorts were generated differently and features were calculated differently, I do not conclude that the increase in ARI from 0.710 to 0.935 represents an improvement over the historical result.

Repeating the assignment of patients to groups is essentially a different issue than validating the accuracy of a phenotype. The equal-domain scaling produced substantial changes in patient assignments based upon their performance on the various tests included in the cognitive battery (ARI = 0.243 versus robust-scaling) and reduced assignment agreement across imputations. Also, there were varying amounts of missing data in several of the memory and executive function tests for some groups. Regardless of how reliable a particular partition is, it may still be affected by either the geometric characteristics of the distribution of scores for the tests administered or other factors associated with how the tests were administered. One advantage of having a strong language-test contrast in one of the robust-space groups is its descriptive utility; however, a clinical interpretation would need to be evaluated under additional measurement and missingness models.

Likelihood results from these analyses also provide a further perspective on the comparisons. Although the restricted $t$ mixtures were able to better fit the incomplete observations than did the selected Gaussian alternatives, as well as favoring eight components at the search boundary (as did the Gaussian models), neither family provided sufficient evidence to support the conclusion of a particular optimal number of patient types. Moreover, although there appeared to be some increase in the likelihood of trajectories with increasing numbers of classes, no multi-class solution could be identified that would satisfy the criteria for a minimal number of members in each class. Therefore, neither analysis can refute the possibility of the existence of very small subgroups.

These results address several issues associated with recently developed methodologies for dealing with incomplete data, ensembles, and longitudinal data \parencite{tong2025mixturemissing,tomo2026ensemble,julia2026longitudinal}, that employed many of the ideas presented in this study. Nevertheless, both the implementation and cohort differ from those in the referenced studies. Thus, this study supports the idea that reproducible cognitive score-based subgroupings exist when certain analytical conditions are met; however, this study does not provide support for clinically distinct phenotypes. It appears that a comparison using independent outcomes of continuous scores alone versus scores plus group membership, followed by external replication, would be required to determine whether identifying patients into pre-specified groups provides meaningful additional information.
