\section{Exploratory Data Analysis}
In addition to what has been stated before in Chapter \ref{Chapter3}, when using tier-1 filtering and removing all assessments that do not include some type of cognitive measure, there were 17,406 assessment records for 7,285 patients. The resulting data matrix contains 14 cognitive variables that fall into 6 different domains. This is listed out in a table format in Table \ref{fig:missingness_barplot} showing the percent missing for each variable.

There is considerable variation in the amount of missing information. The percentages of missingness vary from 0.7\% on the Orientation Items to 27.8\% on the ATMTA. Were only complete cases to be used, 46.9\% of the assessments would be excluded from analysis with a resultant potential for biased selection \parencite{rubin1976inference}. To prevent loss of such a significant number of assessment samples in the eligible population, I decided to perform an imputation.

Although a co-occurring heatmap (not included) did show that there are times when multiple variables are missing at the same time, it does not provide evidence of missing-at-random mechanisms \parencite{rubin1976inference,little2019statistical}. These dependencies suggest that conditional imputation may be superior to naive substitution. Generally speaking, correlation coefficients among the various variables in a given domain are relatively high when compared to correlation coefficients among variables in other domains. High shared variances among the various cognitive measures are compatible with a general cognitive-ability component \parencite{lezak2012neuropsychological} which the subsequent clustering analyses examine further.

Prior to determining how well each cluster model performs on each data set, I first compared each of the ten different imputation models (as presented in Figures \ref{fig:imputation_comparison} and \ref{fig:imputation_comparison_b}). The reason that mean imputation differs from the remaining 9 methods is that mean imputation loses variance when it replaces missing values with the mean \parencite{rubin1987multiple}, while the remaining 9 methods provide closer approximations of the original marginal distributions. In this comparison, KNN and PMM appear to maintain more of the visually discernible discrete structure than do methods like MICE, MissForest and EM, which are producing smooth continuous reconstructions.

\section{Baseline and Primary Clustering}
First, I ran K-means and Gaussian mixture models as baseline approaches to identify the number of groups (\(k\)) from \(k=2\) to \(k=6\). Neither method revealed a clearly defined optimal number of groups. There was no distinct "elbow" identified by the K-means approach. Additionally, the maximum silhouette score for all values of \(k\) was found at \(k=2\), with scores decreasing as the number of groups increased. These results motivated an investigation of density; they were not evidence that natural cognitive groups existed.

I chose HDBSCAN next due to its ability to locate densely packed areas which could take on irregular shapes, classify sparse points as noise, and assess cluster stability across a hierarchical tree, providing a measure of cluster consistency without needing a pre-specified number of clusters (\(k\)). This seemed suited to routine clinical data, where boundary cases may not fit cleanly into a group. As part of my analysis, I ran UMAP using 10 random seed values for each set of parameters, and evaluated HDBSCAN using the full 120 combinations of parameters as previously described in Chapter \ref{Chapter3}. Figure \ref{fig:tsne_umap} shows the selected assignments under t-SNE and UMAP projections. However, it does not serve as an independent means to determine if clusters exist and/or if those clusters are distinct.

\begin{sloppypar}
The final UMAP + HDBSCAN parameters included \texttt{n\_components}=8 and \texttt{n\_neighbors}=15 for UMAP, along with \texttt{min\_dist}=0.0 to allow for tight clustering within the embedding space \parencite{mcinnes2018umap}. When utilizing HDBSCAN, I employed \texttt{min\_cluster\_size}=1000 and \texttt{min\_samples}=50. I used \texttt{cluster\_selection\_method}=eom and \texttt{epsilon}=0.0. I set \texttt{min\_cluster\_size} to a high level to ensure that only substantive groupings would remain.
\end{sloppypar}

I have plotted the mean cognitive-domain scores by tier as z-scores relative to the cohort mean using the radar plot. The three polygons are almost concentric, with all domains moving together; thus it appears that the primary difference among tiers lies in their global levels of functioning rather than their pattern of deficits or strengths.

Additionally, I produced a random forest-based surrogate model which made an attempt at replicating the tier classification based on the individual domain scores. I used Gini importance to measure the degree to which each domain contributed to classifying participants into one of the three groups (tier classifications). The domains are correlated, and the tier labels were derived from the same scores. Consequently, my results indicated that the lower Gini-importance value assigned to "attention" when compared to "orientation", "visuoperception", and "language" does not indicate that attention is clinically less relevant. My findings are limited to the specific tier classification modeled by this particular fit.

\section{Hypothesis 1: Stability and Discreteness}\label{sec:h1_results}
The first claim of hypothesis one is that there are replicable (reproducible) groups within the assessment data. The second claim of hypothesis one is that these subgroups are distinct (discrete) cognitive phenotypes. To assess whether or not the clusters were stable, I performed a total of 50 bootstrap samples per imputation type. For every sample I then fit HDBSCAN to the new sample of the UMAP embeddings, and calculated three metrics: silhouette, pre-KNN noise proportion, and Hennig core-Jaccard matching. I recorded the proportion of noise prior to the KNN assignment step. The metric values for each of the 50 samples created a bootstrapped distribution for the metrics for each type of imputation, as illustrated by Figure \ref{fig:h1_bootstrap}. These distributions allowed me to compare how similar/different the different types of imputations were from one another; this is summarized by the exact details in Table \ref{tab:h1_per_method}.

All ten imputation methods met the thresholds for silhouette and pre-KNN noise proportion. However, there was relatively weak evidence supporting strong, cluster-by-cluster reproducibility. Core Jaccard similarity scores ranged from 0.509 to 0.630 overall and individual matched-cluster similarities ranged from 0.383 to 0.633. As such, none of the methods reached the 0.70 criterion for stability. Moreover, the number of identified clusters differed across many of the methods as well (i.e., three to five). Representations also influenced separation based upon the MICE labeling method. For instance, while selecting the UMAP embedding produced a full coverage silhouette score of 0.526, using the six-domain space with robust scaling reduced it to 0.079. Cluster profiles were concentric, and the first principal component was dominant. Overall, the results appear to demonstrate a reproducible ordering by severity, but do not provide clear-cut distinctions between natural classes. Therefore, I find H1 to be partially supported. While it seems plausible to extract severity groups/strata from data, the level of distinctiveness of the strata is still unclear. Because the bootstrap resampled assessments rather than patients, repeated assessments may have increased apparent stability and reduced uncertainty around the estimates.

\section{Hypothesis 2: Imputation Sensitivity Under a Fixed Partition}\label{sec:h2_results}
I have also tested Hypothesis 2. This hypothesis stated that when you use the same partial data set in an identically designed imputation environment, it can reasonably be expected that the results of clusterings based upon the data sets developed through an identically defined process, will show substantial similarities under a fixed embedding and partition. Using UMAP + HDBSCAN, I found all three primary tiers in my main analyses. To conduct my second level of sensitivity analysis, I performed a StandardScaler, then a UMAP, then a K-Means labeling rule with \(k=3\) on the MICE-imputed domain scores. I did this on each of the other imputed data sets. Because I had already fit my models to the MICE-imputed domain scores, I could only vary the imputed values. Therefore, I matched up all possible methods so that I had 45 pairs of methods. My average ARI was 0.710 and my median ARI was 0.739. The lowest ARI value I obtained was 0.509 while the highest ARI value I obtained was 0.880. The mean consensus fraction was 0.929. There were at least eight out of ten methods that agreed on how they assigned members to their respective clusters in 86.2\% of all assessments.

Therefore, as it relates to H2 and as stated, the assignments of members to clusters are relatively stable across many methods used to replace missing values within an identified single embedding and partitioning model. However, this does not confirm whether or not multiple independently fitted pipelines constructed via UMAP + HDBSCAN will identify similar hierarchical densities. The numbers of clusters generated for each complete-pipeline solution ranged from \(k=3\) to \(k=5\). The numbers of clusters produced in the five separate MICE complete-pipeline runs ranged from \(k=3\) to \(k=7\). This type of variability is consistent with treating a continuum as if it were discrete; however, it is not sufficient to prove this characterization.

\section{Hypothesis 3: Clinical Unit Association}\label{sec:h3_results}
Hypothesis 3 assesses if there are any associations between the cognitive-severity tier and clinical-unit assignment. A chi-square test demonstrated a statistically significant relationship between the two ($\chi^2 = 911.3$, $p \approx 4 \times 10^{-156}$), with Cramér's \(V=0.162\). This is a small effect under Cohen's descriptive guidelines, but exceeds the study's specified cutoff of 0.10. In addition, when calculating the mean Cramér's \(V\) over five independent MICE imputations, a similar average of 0.154 was calculated. Therefore, this represents a summary of sensitivity estimates across multiple imputations rather than an inference based on Rubin's pooling rules.

A stratified sensitivity analysis also found that the association persisted even after controlling for the broad diagnostic grouping which is provided in the clinical-unit lookup. Since these diagnostic groupings were created using the same lookup data that provides the detailed unit assignments, this result should not be considered to represent any form of independent predictive validity.

Patient-grouped multinomial models gave no clear evidence that tier would improve classification of the detailed clinical units beyond the broad diagnostic grouping. Log loss for diagnosis alone was 0.6064, while log loss including tier was 0.6051, representing a decrease of 0.0013. Its 95\% patient-bootstrap interval was $[-0.0006,0.0031]$, which includes zero. The accuracy scores were 0.7772 and 0.7770, respectively, and macro-F1 scores were 0.476 and 0.480, respectively. As such, I consider H3 to be supported only insofar as it represents an association hypothesis, and does not establish added predictive value.

\section{Hypothesis 4: Feature Engineering}\label{sec:h4_results}
The hypothesis for this comparison was that by reducing redundancy, features of an aggregate domain will be easier to interpret than the separate individual variable approach, but they should maintain separation similar to the separate variable method. I examined cluster separation by comparing silhouette values and compared the quality of the feature spaces as represented by the condition numbers. To measure the amount of multicollinearity in each representation, I calculated the variance inflation factor (VIF).

Average silhouette improved with aggregating tests within domains; it went from 0.473 to 0.481 (see Figure \ref{fig:h4_comparison}). However, there was much larger improvement when examining the conditioning of the feature space. The average VIF dropped from 2.77 to 1.88, and the condition number dropped from 61.7 to 11.8. Large VIFs indicate high levels of multicollinearity among variables. As such, if there are correlated variables influencing distance calculations made by both UMAP and HDBSCAN then smaller VIFs would mean fewer redundant influences on the distances after data has been grouped into a cognitive domain. Additionally, groupings by cognitive domain allow for easier understanding and relating the results to the constructs measured by those tests \parencite{lezak2012neuropsychological}.

The main support for Hypothesis 4 comes from a reduction in redundancy and better numerical conditioning and interpretability. However, there is very little difference in the silhouette scores indicating no substantial improvements in clustering separation.

\section{Feature Importance Analysis}\label{sec:feature_importance}
A random forest surrogate model was constructed based on the MICE-imputed domain scores using the pre-existing tier label values to define the target variable. Cross validation across five folds resulted in an overall accuracy rate of 99.4\%, demonstrating that the boundary partitions derived from the same domain scores can be replicated. Although this demonstrates the reproducibility of partition boundaries, it does not independently establish natural cluster separation or predict clinical outcomes.

The Gini Importance ranking for these domains was: Orientation (0.486); Visuoperception (0.237); Language (0.171); Memory (0.061); Attention (0.028); and Executive Function (0.018). As seen in Figure \ref{fig:feature_importance}, Permutation Importances provided similar ordering for the three top-ranked variables with attention ranked lowest. These results detail which variables reproduce the chosen tier labels. If multiple variables are highly correlated, there will likely be overlapping or substitutable effects among them when assessing their contribution, thus influencing both their ordered ranks \parencite{boulesteix2012overview} and ultimately their relative importance to the prediction. Therefore, even if a predictor has a low surrogate importance value, it does not necessarily indicate that the predictor is clinically unimportant. Additional insights into the severity continuum are presented through the domain profile and principal component analysis in Section \ref{sec:cluster_profiles}.

\section{Robustness Analyses}\label{sec:robustness}
I did additional research to see how robust the hypothesis findings were. First, I generated a baseline using a list-wise deletion approach. Next, I did an additional test called a subsample-stability test. Then, I created \textbf{Bootstrap Confidence Intervals.} Afterward, I ran independent MICE imputations and compared the results to those I had found originally. Additionally, I entered into the model each individual's progression through their tier levels (i.e., each subject's transition in tier level over time) and the amount of time (in days) since the injury. Lastly, I applied an adjustment to each of the 45 pairwise H2 statistical tests to control for testing multiple times.

\textbf{Bootstrap Confidence Intervals.} Using assessment-level resampling, I calculated the average ARI (Adjusted Rand Index) for H2 to be 0.710; 95\% CI [0.702, 0.717] and Cramér's V for H3 to be 0.166; 95\% CI [0.156, 0.175]. For H4, the silhouette gap was -0.002; 95\% CI [-0.021, 0.013]. The H4 confidence interval includes zero, so it appears there is no support for increased separation of individuals based upon this additional comparison. Since these are estimates that are derived separately from the initial findings and need not reflect the same results as the headline numbers, the initial raw-test aggregation used in the supplementary feature comparisons differ from the standardized patient-level process. In addition, because this resampling treats all 17,406 assessments as if they were independent (although they actually came from 7,285 different patients), the intervals may appear tighter than warranted; they do not capture any uncertainty due to resampling patients as units.

\textbf{H2 and Multiple Comparison Correction.} To compare all possible combinations (forty-five total) of cluster assignments using the specified cutoff (ARI > 0.50), I used Holm-Bonferroni adjustment to control family-wise Type I error and Benjamini-Hochberg adjustment to control the false discovery rate. Using one-sided parametric bootstrap p-values for each combination, the null-hypothesis was rejected for forty-four of the forty-five combinations at both family-wise and FDR corrected significance levels. KNN--SoftImpute was the borderline case; its ARI had a mean bootstrap value of 0.509 and 95\% CI of [0.497, 0.521].

\textbf{Across-Imputation Sensitivity Summary.} To assess variability across imputations, I created \(M = 5\) independently generated MICE imputed datasets and completed the full pipeline on each dataset. Across all nonlinear statistics, I present the cross-imputation mean (\(\bar{Q}\)) as well as a descriptive dispersion interval for that statistic defined as \(\bar{Q} \pm t_{0.975,4}\sqrt{(1+1/M)B}\) , where \(B\) represents the sample variance of the five estimates. Since no estimation of the within-imputation variance exists, these intervals represent only the between-imputation variation and should be interpreted as such. Due to the non-linear nature of Cramér's V and silhouette, they do not represent Rubin-rule confidence intervals. Rather, they serve as sensitivity analyses providing summaries of how much the results can vary based upon different plausible sets of imputed data. The overall mean Cramér's V was 0.154 with a descriptive interval of [0.049, 0.259]. The average silhouette gap was 0.050 [0.043, 0.058]. The number of recovered clusters ranged from three to seven. Domain-level features outperformed variable-level features in each of the five runs.

\textbf{Listwise Deletion Baseline.} Following removal of 46.9\% of the total number of assessments containing missing values, I then completed the remainder of the pipeline with the remaining 9,245 complete case assessments. This generated a two-cluster solution with a silhouette measure of 0.257. The MICE cluster solution for these same assessments demonstrated moderate correspondence with the historical solutions derived using only complete case assessments (ARI = 0.625; NMI = 0.519). However, they are not identical. As such, the use of imputation does affect how one conducts their analysis; removing 46.9\% of assessments may result in differing numbers or qualities of levels/tiers as defined by this study.

\textbf{Subsampling Stability.} Across the twenty 80 percent random samples of assessments used in this study, on average, there was an ARI value of 0.273 among pairs of fits. The median pairwise ARI was 0.277. Fifteen fits produced a solution that consisted of two clusters, while three fits found a solution consisting of three clusters. Two fits did not produce any type of clustering solution. As such, the historical UMAP + HDBSCAN tiering assignment has considerable sensitivity based upon resampling. Results presented here relate to assessment rows and do not relate to the patient level k-means stability results discussed in Section \ref{sec:extension_results}.

\textbf{Missingness Threshold Sensitivity.} I repeated the full process of variable selection, domain computation, MICE imputation and UMAP + HDBSCAN clustering using five Tier-1 thresholds from 30\% to 70\%. As Figure \ref{fig:sensitivity} shows, at thresholds from 40\% through 70\%, the tier number ranged from two to three, while the silhouette scores ranged between 0.39 and 0.52. However, once a threshold of 30\% was applied to the analysis, the restriction placed upon the variables resulted in a failure of the system. Therefore, the historical threshold sweep provided here will evaluate how sensitive the selected variables and resultant pipeline are and not whether the primary saved matrix was produced using a 50\% row-missingness limit. The established row rule is described in Section \ref{sec:filtering}.

\textbf{Time Since Injury and Tier Transitions.} The median time since injury for individuals in the Above-Average group is 214 days, 188 days for individuals in the Near-Normal group, and 172 days for those classified as Global Impairment. Due to their nature, cross-sectional comparisons do not allow for an assessment of an individual's overall level of recovery and should never be considered as an independent measure of functional status. Out of a total of 5,206 patients who had at least one repeat assessment, there were 2,613 (or roughly 50.2\%) whose tier classification did not change during any of their observed visits, while there were 2,593 (roughly 49.8\%) whose tier classifications changed. Among these patients, 912 had exactly two assessments and experienced a change in tier classification: 706 of whom moved to a higher-tiered classification and 206 of whom moved to a lower-tiered classification. This represents movement within cognitive-score bands but does not establish validated functional improvement. Factors such as timing of visit; discharge; and availability of tests may have affected observed tier transitions.

\textbf{Initialization and Resampling.} The historical parameter search repeats UMAP initialization. The choice of a seed based upon the silhouette value of the seed as a part of model selection is not an independent validation. Above are assessments of a second (and separate) source of variation (subsampling at the assessment level). The patient-level extension reports initialization agreement and patient-subsampling agreement separately for its fitted \(k\)-means partition.

\textbf{Gaussian Mixture / Latent Profile Baseline.} In order to provide a statistical basis for comparison among the different classification techniques, I applied Gaussian mixture modeling (using full covariance matrices) to the imputed domain scores for \(k = 2\) through 6. Specifically, this represents one way to conduct a type of latent profile analysis. However, since the analyses were based on the imputed data, they do not represent the traditional latent profile analysis using full-information maximum likelihood estimation on the raw and incomplete data. As \(k\) increases, the Bayesian information criterion (BIC) decreases from 243,249 for \(k\)=2 down to 92,606 for \(k\)=6. No Bayesian information criterion optima were identified across the various levels of \(k\) that were analyzed. While this does not establish a continuum and potentially a better fit would occur if additional components existed beyond those tested (i.e., greater than 6), the level of agreement between the tiered densities based on the methods described above and the results obtained by the mixture-modeling techniques were generally moderate (ARI ranged from 0.350 to 0.400); however, the two-component solution produced the highest value (0.45) for silhouette. Consequently, I consider the mixture-modeling procedures to produce ambiguous answers relative to the number of categories. The continuum representation has many of its characteristics dependent upon the concentric nature of the domain profiles, the dominance of the first principal component, weak original-space silhouettes and the dependency of the number of tiers on the technique used. The observed-data mixture extension in Section \ref{sec:extension_results} fits the incomplete test matrix directly, using a wider range of restricted distributional families.

\textbf{Missing-Not-at-Random Sensitivity.} While there was agreement among the different imputation techniques used, it is still dependent on what each technique assumed regarding the missing data. No studies I conducted comparing the sensitivity of all the methods revealed that the results would remain unaffected by potentially informative missingness with respect to test scores. Thus, as part of my future research, I will conduct a formally defined delta-shift sensitivity analysis that respects test direction and uses clinically justified shifts.

\textbf{Assessment Eligibility.} A total of 17,406 of 22,075 records contained at least one observed score among the 14 retained tests; these 17,406 records were thus analyzable. Records without measurable responses from the 14 tests in this study (i.e., n = 4,669) were excluded from analysis. The absence of retained test values in those encounters prevents direct assessment of cognitive severity. However, that does not indicate that excluding them is harmless from a clinical standpoint.

\section{Cluster Clinical Naming}\label{sec:cluster_names}
I applied domain-level z-score profiles to establish a rule-based system that would assign clinically relevant labels to density-based clusters. There are three possible labels assigned to each cluster profile.

Profiles with domain z-scores greater than +0.5 were classified as "Above Average."

Profiles with domain z-scores less than -0.5 were labeled "Global Impairment."

All remaining cluster profiles were classified as "Near Normal" or the middle category.

There were 6,725 "Above Average," 6,328 "Near Normal," and 4,353 "Global Impairment" assessments in the total assessments.

These bands have different average values but similar shape. As such, severity labels are better than phenotype names.

They provide a shorthand description of the historical assessment-level partition. Clinical utility for triage decisions has not been established for this label assignment, nor do they transfer automatically to the new patient-level groups.

In addition to providing assessment counts and domain profiles for each band, CogDash also displays the separate patient-level findings. Group numbers are specific to each fitted solution.
